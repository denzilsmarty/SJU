## Overview

The Ignatian Pedagogical Paradigm (IPP) at Saint Joseph's University (SJU) is not a neutral framework applied to a generic learner. Its five dynamics—context, experience, reflection, action, and evaluation—are built on an insistence that teaching must begin with *the actual, concrete person*. Inclusion and universal design are therefore not additions bolted onto the paradigm; they are intrinsic requirements that follow directly from its foundational commitments, especially *cura personalis* and the dynamic of context.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## 1. Inclusion as Intrinsic to Context and Cura Personalis

The IPP's dynamic of **context** requires educators to attend to the learner's prior knowledge, experiences, feelings, values, readiness, social relationships, culture, and material conditions—as well as the institutional setting and wider environment.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] This is not a demographic preface completed once at the start of a semester. It is an ongoing inquiry into what makes this learning possible, difficult, meaningful, risky, or distorted for *these* learners.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

The SJU source document states this plainly:

> "The IPP requires attention to the actual learner and therefore to disability, race, gender, economic condition, culture, language, religion, age, prior education, and other dimensions that shape access and experience. Inclusion is not an external addition to an otherwise complete paradigm. It corrects abstractions that treat one student experience as universal."[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

**Cura personalis**—care for the whole, concrete person—is the relational commitment that makes this contextual attention possible. It requires knowledge of learners, trust, appropriate challenge, and adaptation to their actual situations. It is explicitly characterized as an enabling condition for the paradigm, not a separate step or simply "being nice."[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

### Context questions that surface inclusion concerns

The IPP source document provides a set of diagnostic questions educators should ask when attending to context. Several directly implicate inclusion and access:[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

- What linguistic, cultural, financial, disability-related, family, work, or caregiving conditions affect participation?
- What identities and experiences are salient, and which should not be presumed?
- What histories and power relations shape the subject and the classroom?
- What does the discipline count as evidence, and who has historically been authorized to produce it?
- What institutional policies, technologies, schedules, and incentives structure the experience?
- What support, accessibility, consent, and safeguarding conditions are necessary?

The document also names useful contextual practices: prior-knowledge probes, low-stakes diagnostic work, transparent learning purposes, learner profiles that protect privacy, accessibility checks, community agreements, and analysis of disciplinary history.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

### Guardrail: Context must not become stereotyping

Context can become stereotyping if educators infer beliefs or capacity from group identity. It can become voyeurism if students are pressed to disclose trauma or private experience. The proper standard is **informed adaptation with high expectations and appropriate support**.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## 2. Universal Design for Learning and the IPP

The SJU source document explicitly aligns the IPP with Universal Design for Learning (UDL), noting that UDL's commitment to **multiple means of engagement, representation, and expression** widens access in ways consonant with the paradigm.[^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0] The document distinguishes the two frameworks as complementary rather than identical:

| Framework | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Universal Design for Learning** | Offers more specific design guidance for accessible instructional materials and methods |
| **IPP** | Supplies a wider formative horizon—attending to why methods are chosen and toward what human and educational ends |

[^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0]

The practical synthesis is that IPP-informed educators should draw on UDL's design specificity *within* the IPP's recursive, relational frame. The IPP asks *why* and *toward what ends* methods are selected; learning science and disciplinary research guide how reliably they work.[^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0]

### What this looks like in course design

The source document states that inclusive design—accessible materials, multiple appropriate ways to engage and demonstrate learning, transparent expectations, and responsive support—**strengthens, not dilutes, academic rigor**. Equity does not require identical experience; it requires defensible pathways to the intended learning.[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

The IPP design canvas prompts instructors to ask, for every course or program:[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

- **Context:** What accessibility, consent, privacy, or safeguarding requirements apply? Who are the learners and other affected persons? What prior knowledge, cultures, identities, resources, and constraints matter?
- **Experience:** What preparation and support are necessary? How will learners be required to *notice* rather than merely attend?
- **Reflection:** How will reflection avoid compulsory disclosure and predetermined agreement? Where will alternative modes—silence, dialogue, critique, revision—occur?
- **Action:** Is action proportionate to the learner's knowledge, role, and power? Who participates in defining it, and who bears its risks?
- **Evaluation:** What consequences, inequities, and unintended effects will be reviewed?

## 3. Multiple Means of Engagement: IPP Requirements

The IPP does not enumerate a list of required engagement modalities in UDL's technical sense, but it establishes requirements that functionally demand multiple means of engagement.

### Experience must be accessible and appropriated

Strong experience in an IPP-informed course must be:[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

- aligned to a significant learning purpose;
- authentic enough to reveal the complexity of the subject;
- **accessible and safe enough for meaningful participation**;
- prepared by necessary knowledge and skill;
- structured so learners must notice rather than merely attend;
- capable of engaging appropriate affect without exploiting it; and
- followed by reflection, response, and feedback.

The category of "experience" is deliberately wide. A student's sustained encounter with a difficult theorem, poem, clinical case, data set, historical archive, or artistic performance can all be Ignatian experience if it is intellectually real, personally appropriated, and open to reflection.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] This breadth is itself a form of universal design: no single modality of engagement is privileged as the only authentic encounter with a subject.

### Reflection need not be written or personal

The source document explicitly states that reflection may be **analytic, dialogic, oral, visual, quantitative, embodied, or design-based**. Personal appropriation matters, but disclosure is not required.[^chunk:514c92fa-878f-5bb4-b7dd-a668e30ffd19] Good prompts connect personal response to evidence and disciplinary standards; they also offer options for private or impersonal reflection.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

### Action must fit the discipline, role, and context

Action is not limited to service or advocacy. It includes interior choice, revised professional judgment, new research questions, design decisions, and civic responses.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] The document warns that "action" should not reward students who have more time, money, mobility, or institutional access, and that alternatives can preserve learning outcomes.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] This is a direct equity requirement: learning designs must not systematically disadvantage learners based on material or social circumstance.

## 4. Safeguarding Freedom and Privacy in Inclusive Practice

Reflective education can cross boundaries if it confuses depth with disclosure. The source document sets out clear requirements for SJU practice:[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

- Distinguish academic reflection from therapy, confession, and spiritual direction.
- Never require disclosure of trauma, diagnosis, immigration status, faith practice, or other sensitive information unless essential, lawful, and appropriately protected.
- Offer **alternative prompts** that preserve the learning objective.
- Explain who will read reflective work and how it will be used.
- Establish referral and crisis protocols.
- Train educators to respond without assuming clinical or pastoral roles beyond their competence.

No student may be required to simulate belief or disclose private spiritual experience. People of every faith and of no religious affiliation are treated as participants, not mission objects.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## 5. Academic Freedom, Viewpoint Diversity, and Inclusion

Inclusion in the IPP framework encompasses intellectual pluralism as well as identity and access. The source document specifies that SJU's standards should focus on evidence, reasoning, scholarly norms, dignity, and the conditions for genuine dialogue—not on policing conclusions or requiring conformity to a faculty member's preferred position.[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

Faculty should not claim to measure the state of a student's conscience, compassion, faith, political virtue, or human worth. Assessable evidence includes the specificity of observation; use of relevant concepts; acknowledgment of uncertainty; examination of assumptions; engagement with affected perspectives; proportionality of response; revision after feedback; and analysis of consequences. Rubrics should describe **observable qualities in work, not identity claims about the person**.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## 6. Inclusion in the IPP Evaluation Rubric

The SJU design-review rubric includes an explicit equity dimension within evaluation. A *strong* design is one in which mastery, judgment, consequence, **equity**, and design are reviewed—and feedback begins a new cycle.[^chunk:514c92fa-878f-5bb4-b7dd-a668e30ffd19] An *emerging* design reduces evaluation to grades or character claims without attending to differential effects or unintended burdens.

Similarly, a *strong* context dimension in the rubric means that learner, disciplinary, social, institutional, and **power** contexts shape the design—not merely that some prior knowledge needs are acknowledged.[^chunk:514c92fa-878f-5bb4-b7dd-a668e30ffd19]

The source document specifies that the SJU evidence agenda should look for **differential effects and unintended burdens**, combining belonging and access data alongside student work, qualitative accounts, partner feedback, and institutional indicators.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## 7. Institutional Responsibility

Inclusion is not only a course-level concern. The IPP holds that **the institution teaches**: policies, incentives, technologies, spaces, workloads, partnerships, and decision processes form people as surely as syllabi do.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] One of SJU's eight proposed institutional commitments under the IPP is explicitly:

> "Justice and the common good: context and consequence include excluded people, structural conditions, and creation."[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

Another is **institutional congruence**: policies, resources, incentives, and technologies must support the educational commitments they proclaim.[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940] An institution cannot credibly claim an inclusive paradigm while maintaining policies that systematically disadvantage particular learners.

A Living Tradition (2019) names **accessibility** among its ten global identifiers for Jesuit schools, alongside Catholic identity and faith dialogue, safety, global citizenship, care for creation, justice, interculturality, networking, human excellence, and lifelong learning.[^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0]

## Summary Table: Where Inclusion Appears Across the Five Dynamics

| Dynamic | Inclusion Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Context** | Attend to disability, race, gender, economics, culture, language, religion, age, power, and history; avoid stereotyping; conduct accessibility checks |
| **Experience** | Ensure experiences are accessible and safe for meaningful participation; provide preparation and support; avoid designs that reward social privilege |
| **Reflection** | Offer multiple modes (oral, written, visual, etc.); never compel disclosure; provide alternative prompts; explain evaluation criteria |
| **Action** | Proportion action to learner's role, knowledge, and power; provide alternatives that preserve learning outcomes; avoid penalizing material disadvantage |
| **Evaluation** | Review for equity, differential effects, and unintended burdens; assess quality of reasoning, not moral worth or belief |
