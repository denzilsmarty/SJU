# Justice, Social Apostolate, and Formation of Conscience

The Justice, Social Apostolate, and Formation of Conscience section encompasses the comprehensive commitment of Jesuit institutions to integrate faith with action for justice, grounded in Ignatian spirituality and expressed through educational mission, research, ecological stewardship, and witness to the Gospel. This section addresses how Jesuit universities form conscience-driven leaders, conduct justice-oriented research, serve the poor and vulnerable, and collaborate globally in pursuit of structural transformation.

## Jesuit Institutional Commitment to Social Justice

The Society of Jesus developed a distinctive institutional commitment to social justice through progressive clarification of its apostolic mission. Fr. General Janssens issued a historic letter to the entire Society in 1949 regarding the social apostolate, marking an important moment in the Society's explicit commitment to justice work.[^chunk:6d064983-ced2-5ba2-9ccd-9ced6fe112aa] The most significant institutional turning point came with General Congregation 32 (GC 32) in 1975, when the Society formally declared that "the mission of the Society of Jesus today is the service of faith, of which the promotion of justice is an absolute requirement."[^chunk:6d064983-ced2-5ba2-9ccd-9ced6fe112aa]

## Faith That Does Justice: Jesuit Educational Mission

"Faith that does justice" represents a central commitment of Jesuit universities, expressing the inseparable unity between service of faith and promotion of justice.[^chunk:6d57e7d9-44bd-5906-8506-1269f2caa002] This concept emerged from General Congregation 32, which redefined the Jesuit mission as the "service of faith and the promotion of justice."[^chunk:253fa195-ca17-5646-9405-e38cbe6f3b98] Pedro Arrupe, Superior General of the Society of Jesus, articulated the foundational concept of Jesuit education as forming "men and women for others."[^chunk:a664b19a-af4f-5620-9c55-351d7f3b3e16]

## Service to the Poor and Vulnerable

Jesuit universities embed service to the poor through multiple institutional mechanisms. The foundational approach involves integrating a "perspective of the poor" into all academic curricula.[^chunk:90787dfa-d610-5af8-a071-48544d40fafd] The Catholic University of Cordoba, Argentina, exemplifies this institutional commitment by requiring that "all departments should include a solidarity perspective related to social situations in their subjects in at least 10% of the contents," while investing university resources in financing these experiences and providing economic stimulus to professors who implement them.[^chunk:90787dfa-d610-5af8-a071-48544d40fafd]

Beyond curriculum, universities offer programs connecting academic study to direct service by placing students in contact with "persons or situations which present them with an ethical and emotional dilemma (indigenous peoples, migrants, prisoners, street children, substance abusers, the sick…). These programs ordinarily include close accompaniment of the student and adequate time for reflection on the experience."[^chunk:250e2d93-005e-57db-ae12-12926dd08952]

## Justice-Oriented Research and University Mission

Justice-oriented research represents a fundamental reorientation of university scholarship toward serving the poor and addressing structural inequality. Ignacio Ellacuría, SJ articulated a distinctive vision for university research: "The university should incarnate itself among the poor so as to become science for those who have no science, an informed voice of those who have no voice, and intellectual backing for those who in their lived reality know truth and right but lack academic arguments that could justify and legitimize their truth and right."[^chunk:2097a7d6-37db-5fbd-aff2-b1c756502acb]

Fr. Kolvenbach emphasized that "it is not truly neutral because it always carries with it the bias of the values and particular conceptual structure of human beings."[^chunk:2097a7d6-37db-5fbd-aff2-b1c756502acb] Rather than serving existing power structures, justice-oriented research deliberately positions itself "from the perspective of the poor for the sake of bettering their lives, for it is in their suffering that the inhumanity of unjust structures becomes clearly manifest."[^chunk:2097a7d6-37db-5fbd-aff2-b1c756502acb]

## Ecological Care and Stewardship of Creation

Ecological care represents a fundamental dimension of the Jesuit mission in the 21st century. The Fourth Universal Apostolic Preference (2019-2029) explicitly commits the Society of Jesus to "collaborate in the care of our Common Home."[^chunk:f5f709f2-8d18-5c9b-bca7-89f2844b7e7a] General Congregation 35 (GC 35) confirmed that "the promotion of justice includes a commitment to care for creation," establishing ecology as "an integral part of our mission."[^chunk:6d57e7d9-44bd-5906-8506-1269f2caa002]

The Jesuit commitment to ecological care is inseparable from the commitment to social justice. The encyclical *Laudato Si'* articulates this connection, noting that "the damage done to the earth is also damage done to the most vulnerable, such as indigenous peoples, peasants forced to emigrate, and the inhabitants of urban peripheries."[^chunk:f5f709f2-8d18-5c9b-bca7-89f2844b7e7a]

## Universal Apostolic Preferences and Mission Renewal

Universal Apostolic Preferences represent fundamental strategic orientations guiding the mission and apostolic direction of Jesuit institutions globally. Very Rev. Arturo Sosa, S.J., Superior General of the Society of Jesus, has emphasized that these preferences constitute "basic orientations for our life-mission in the next ten years" and open "a new stage of our journey towards a better understanding and practice of the care of mission and people."[^chunk:6f4937fe-63ab-5d7e-b5d0-240d7e30ca51]

The Mission Priority Examen (MPE) represents a key institutional mechanism through which Jesuit colleges and universities operationalize these universal preferences. The process requires each institution to address foundational questions: "Do you want to continue to be a Jesuit, Catholic University? If so, what are the two to four mission goals (and accompanying strategies) that you will prioritize for the next few years?"[^chunk:b4c6be36-f526-5906-bb00-5bd39e971bf5]

## Formation of Conscience-Driven Leaders

Jesuit higher education seeks to form students into leaders of conscience who are committed to working for justice in society. The foundation of this formation lies in what has been called the "Ledesma-Kolvenbach educational paradigm," which emphasizes four essential qualities: utility, justice, humanism, and faith.[^chunk:6d064983-ced2-5ba2-9ccd-9ced6fe112aa] Justice enables students to "contribute to the proper governance of public affairs and the appropriate formulation of laws."[^chunk:6d064983-ced2-5ba2-9ccd-9ced6fe112aa]

The real measure of Jesuit universities lies not in what students accomplish academically, but "in who our students become."[^chunk:53c7f0e0-1c21-525b-878f-68989478ecef] Universities must raise their educational standard to "educate the whole person of solidarity for the real world."[^chunk:53c7f0e0-1c21-525b-878f-68989478ecef] This formation of solidarity is learned through direct contact and experience rather than abstract concepts; "when the heart is touched by direct experience, the mind may be challenged to change."[^chunk:53c7f0e0-1c21-525b-878f-68989478ecef]

Jesuit universities recognize that students have different capacities for commitment to social responsibility and therefore provide specialized attention to those "from whom more can be expected."[^chunk:28130dc0-eb1b-5ed7-9a23-2969857c90b7]

## Global Networks and Collaborative Initiatives

Jesuit universities worldwide are connected through collaborative networks designed to amplify their social justice commitments. The Association of Jesuit Universities of Latin America (AUSJAL) has been structuring networks for over 20 years with significant progress.[^chunk:78db57c0-d9f6-5b27-af7d-6d4094c88d71] AUSJAL has developed homologous networks bringing together academicians from similar fields across different universities to exchange information and best practices, and established an "Observatory of Poverty" for comparative study of poverty and its evolution across Latin America.[^chunk:78db57c0-d9f6-5b27-af7d-6d4094c88d71]

Other regional collaborative networks include AJCU (United States), JHESA (South Asia), and UNIJES (Spain), which have been active for decades and now offer support for international collaboration.[^chunk:78db57c0-d9f6-5b27-af7d-6d4094c88d71] Loyola University Chicago exemplifies international coordination efforts by sponsoring a network of approximately forty researchers from Jesuit institutions around the world focusing on ecological concerns, and sponsoring a three-year international research project on "Democracy, Culture and Catholicism" involving six Jesuit universities worldwide.[^chunk:78db57c0-d9f6-5b27-af7d-6d4094c88d71]

## Witness and Martyrdom in the Pursuit of Justice

On November 16, 1989, at the Universidad Centroamericana of El Salvador, six Jesuit priests and two women were assassinated because of their commitment to peace and justice during El Salvador's civil war. The Jesuits killed were Ignacio Ellacuría, Ignacio Martín-Baró, Segundo Montes, Juan Ramón Moreno, Amando López, and Joaquín López, along with Elba Ramos and Celina Ramos, who worked for their community.[^chunk:9698f13c-eb7d-54b7-be45-2fd5c65d4bdb]

These priests "had striven to make the university an institution that defended El Salvador's poor majority, and it was their decision to help the poor that cost them their lives."[^chunk:9698f13c-eb7d-54b7-be45-2fd5c65d4bdb] Their deaths exemplified the ultimate consequence of the Jesuit commitment to "faith that does justice."[^chunk:9698f13c-eb7d-54b7-be45-2fd5c65d4bdb]

The Jesuit universities of the Society can claim these martyrs as their own because they were "scholars and teachers who were killed for their commitment to the 'faith that does justice.'"[^chunk:9698f13c-eb7d-54b7-be45-2fd5c65d4bdb] Their witness stands as a powerful testimony to the cost of authentic engagement with social justice in the university context.
