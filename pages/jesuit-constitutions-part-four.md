# The Constitutions of the Society of Jesus: Part IV and Educational Framework

## Overview

Part IV of the Constitutions of the Society of Jesus, composed by Ignatius of Loyola between 1547–1552 and approved in 1550–1552, represents the foundational framework governing Jesuit education and the development of learning within the Society.[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572] This section of the Constitutions, titled "The Instruction of Those Who Remain in the Society as Members, in Letters and in Other Means of Helping Their Fellow Men," establishes the educational mission that has shaped Jesuit pedagogy for nearly five centuries.[^chunk:629f9459-409a-533c-adac-9c8e5e9298c3]

## Ignatius of Loyola's Educational Vision

Ignatius of Loyola, born in the Basque province of Guipuzcoa probably in 1491, received his Master of Arts degree from the University of Paris in Lent of 1534 at age forty-three.[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572] His vision for Jesuit education emerged from the Society's foundational purpose: to aid both its own members and their fellow men in attaining the ultimate end for which they were created.[^chunk:629f9459-409a-533c-adac-9c8e5e9298c3]

The pedagogical objective was explicit: learning and skill in expounding it were necessary, alongside the example of one's life, to help make God the Creator and Lord better known and served.[^chunk:629f9459-409a-533c-adac-9c8e5e9298c3] However, Ignatius recognized a practical challenge: those of "good and learned" character were relatively few, and most sought rest from their labors. To address this constraint, the Society determined to admit young men whose good habits and talent gave hope they would become both virtuous and learned, rather than restricting membership exclusively to those already fully educated.[^chunk:629f9459-409a-533c-adac-9c8e5e9298c3]

## Composition and Authorship

While Ignatius was the author of all material in the Constitutions, the style is largely that of Father Juan de Polanco of Castile, his secretary.[^chunk:6ad612e4-d2d0-5f75-b6e1-c7b7d9f783d3] Ignatius wrote in his native Spanish but examined minutely what Polanco wrote, often making verbal changes.[^chunk:6ad612e4-d2d0-5f75-b6e1-c7b7d9f783d3] In composing the material about universities in Part Four, Ignatius accepted counsel from Fathers James Lainez, Juan de Polanco, and André des Freux.[^chunk:6ad612e4-d2d0-5f75-b6e1-c7b7d9f783d3]

## Main Provisions of Part IV

### Preamble and Foundation

The preamble to Part IV establishes that after proper foundation in self-abnegation and growth in virtues, members must be trained in letters and in the manner of employing them as a help to know and serve God better.[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572] The Society undertakes colleges and universities where those who prove worthy but lack necessary learning may be instructed in both studies and other means of helping souls.[^chunk:629f9459-409a-533c-adac-9c8e5e9298c3]

### Colleges and Their Purpose

Part IV systematically addresses the colleges' foundation, material aspects, student admission and progress, and governance.[^chunk:629f9459-409a-533c-adac-9c8e5e9298c3] A key provision establishes that in colleges of the Society, curacies of souls and obligations to celebrate Masses should not be undertaken, as these distract from study and interfere with the educational aim.[^chunk:5637c890-3421-5def-9821-8581bf652557] The Superior General held full authority to accept colleges freely offered to the Society for administration according to its Constitutions, though considerations about attached conditions could be brought before a General Congregation.[^chunk:629f9459-409a-533c-adac-9c8e5e9298c3]

### Curriculum and Studies

Ignatius specified branches of study essential for Jesuit formation and apostolic work. The curriculum included humane letters of various languages, logic, natural philosophy, moral philosophy, metaphysics, scholastic theology, positive theology, and Sacred Scripture.[^chunk:27f097eb-40cc-5683-b95c-397a2ca7bc0b] Students should devote themselves with greater diligence to parts most helpful for the Society's end, with account taken of circumstances of time, place, persons, and other factors.[^chunk:27f097eb-40cc-5683-b95c-397a2ca7bc0b]

Ignatius emphasized that superiors should decide what each individual member studied according to their ability, noting that some might be sent not to become learned men in the full sense but to help others—such as priests sent to hear confessions.[^chunk:27f097eb-40cc-5683-b95c-397a2ca7bc0b]

### Formation of Students

Schilastics were urged to preserve purity of conscience and right intention in studying, seeking nothing except the glory of God and the good of souls.[^chunk:27f097eb-40cc-5683-b95c-397a2ca7bc0b] Impediments to study—excessive devotions, external occupations, and distracting conversations—should be removed as far as possible.[^chunk:27f097eb-40cc-5683-b95c-397a2ca7bc0b]

### University Governance

Part IV's later chapters (11–17) address university administration, establishing positions including a rector with overall authority, a chancellor distinguished for learning, a secretary, notaries, and beadles.[^chunk:46647514-842e-5cac-98cd-c89c2ac4c108] The university was to be divided into three faculties (languages/humanities, arts/philosophy, and theology), each with a dean and representatives.[^chunk:46647514-842e-5cac-98cd-c89c2ac4c108]

## Remembrance of Founders and Benefactors

Part IV begins with detailed provisions for honoring college founders. Every college was to have a Mass said weekly in perpetuity for its founder and benefactors, with monthly Masses by all priests and an annual solemn Mass on the anniversary of the college's transfer to the Society.[^chunk:629f9459-409a-533c-adac-9c8e5e9298c3] Founders became special sharers in all the good works of the colleges and the whole Society, with the Society bound by obligation of charity to show them service according to its humble profession for divine glory.[^chunk:629f9459-409a-533c-adac-9c8e5e9298c3]

## Promulgation and Development of the Constitutions

In 1551, the substance of the Constitutions was approved by consultants, though Ignatius continued revising them until his death on July 31, 1556.[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572] To keep the Constitutions brief, Ignatius created a separate book of Clarifications and Observations (Declaraciones y Avisos) with equal authority to the main text.[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572]

In spring 1552, Ignatius began experimental promulgation through Father Jerome Nadal, who from June 1552 to September 1554 traversed Sicily, Spain, and Portugal, assembling communities and explaining the Constitutions daily while addressing individual doubts.[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572] On November 1, 1554, Nadal became Ignatius' Vicar-General.[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572]

## Publication History

After Ignatius' death, translation into Latin was pressed forward, with Polanco as the author of the Latin version.[^chunk:6ad612e4-d2d0-5f75-b6e1-c7b7d9f783d3] The First General Congregation (summer 1558) decreed that the Latin translation be published in printed form; the Examen appeared in 1558 and the Constitutions with Clarifications in 1559.[^chunk:6ad612e4-d2d0-5f75-b6e1-c7b7d9f783d3] A second edition with emendations from the Second General Congregation was printed in 1570, a third in 1577, and the fourth edition—decreed by the Fourth General Congregation in 1581 as the authentic and vulgate text—was printed in 1583.[^chunk:6ad612e4-d2d0-5f75-b6e1-c7b7d9f783d3]

## Modifications Through General Congregations

The Constitutions have been subject to ongoing clarification and modification by General Congregations of the Society. The first published Latin translation of 1558–1559 was revised by successive General Congregations, with the Second General Congregation (1570) and Fourth General Congregation (1583) prescribing formal emendations.[^chunk:6ad612e4-d2d0-5f75-b6e1-c7b7d9f783d3]

In modern times, substantial modifications have occurred. General Congregation 34 abolished certain prescriptive norms regarding university governance and students, though it retained them as "appropriate and useful counsels and criteria" for Jesuit higher education apostolate.[^chunk:154b7bea-e7e7-5cb3-91f2-67703a4797c2] Various clarifications have been issued by Congregations Normales and specific General Congregations addressing how certain provisions apply under contemporary conditions.[^chunk:154b7bea-e7e7-5cb3-91f2-67703a4797c2]

## Part IV Within the Complete Constitutions

Part IV occupies a distinct position among the Constitutions' ten parts. It follows Part III (on preservation and improvement of novices) and precedes Part V (on definitive incorporation through final profession).[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572] The complete structure includes: Part I (admission to probation), Part II (dismissal of unsuitable members), Part III (preservation during novitiate), Part IV (instruction in letters and means of helping souls), Part V (final profession), Part VI (persons incorporated), Part VII (distribution for apostolic work), Part VIII (union of scattered members), Part IX (the head and executive power), and Part X (preservation and increase of the Society).[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572]

In Part Four, chapters 7, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, and 17 apply especially to extern students as well as to members of the order.[^chunk:d9dee400-88f7-5a52-a73b-448b861a1572]

## Modern Implementation and Pedagogy

The Jesuit formation tradition organizes itself around six axes: the source-document corpus (including the Constitutions), anchor concepts and vocabulary, pedagogical method, curricular architecture, formation outcomes, and strategic horizon.[^chunk:ee9edb86-4abc-5e21-9216-74016824bd16] The Ignatian Pedagogical Paradigm—context, experience, reflection, action, and evaluation—is the modern pedagogical translation of principles established in Part IV and the Spiritual Exercises.[^chunk:ee9edb86-4abc-5e21-9216-74016824bd16]

The curricular architecture derived from Part IV emphasizes the integration of wisdom, virtue, and eloquence (eloquentia perfecta) across disciplines.[^chunk:ee9edb86-4abc-5e21-9216-74016824bd16] This integration is instantiated in modern Jesuit universities through comprehensive core curricula combining humanities, philosophy, theology, sciences, and social sciences with explicit ethical formation and commitment to justice.[^chunk:ee9edb86-4abc-5e21-9216-74016824bd16]
