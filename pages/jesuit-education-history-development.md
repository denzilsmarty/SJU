## Origins and Early Development

The first Jesuit school opened in Messina, Sicily in 1548[^chunk:d4f703b8-e359-5cf3-a646-0073f5112c5f]. This marked the beginning of a major educational apostolate for the Society of Jesus, which had been formally established just eight years earlier in 1540[^chunk:d4f703b8-e359-5cf3-a646-0073f5112c5f].

The development of Jesuit education was not without internal debate. In the years following the founding, not all Jesuits agreed that involvement in schools was a proper activity for the Society[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]. Despite this resistance, educational work expanded rapidly. Of the 40 schools that Ignatius had personally approved, at least 35 were in operation when he died in 1556, even though the Society's total membership had not yet reached 1,000[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]. Within forty years, the number of Jesuit schools would reach 245[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

Ignatius himself established the Roman College in 1551, intending it to serve as a model for all Jesuit schools throughout the world[^chunk:90cce47f-ffdc-5af2-9abc-edf4dc22d5aa]. In Part IV of the Constitutions, which was revised to include educational directives, Ignatius set the foundational goals for Jesuit education: to aid Society members and "their fellowmen to attain the ultimate end for which they were created" through the means of "learning and a method of expounding it"[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

## Development During the 16th and 17th Centuries

The expansion of Jesuit schools in the 16th and 17th centuries was systematic and carefully coordinated. Following Ignatius's death, successive Jesuit superiors encouraged an exchange of ideas based on concrete experiences so that a common curriculum and basic pedagogy could be developed while respecting the principle that "circumstances of place and persons" should be taken into account[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

The most significant development was the creation of the Ratio Studiorum (Plan of Studies). The first drafts were based on the Rules of the Roman College[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]. An international committee of six Jesuits was appointed by Superior General Claudio Acquaviva to meet in Rome and adapt these rules based on experiences from other parts of the world. Preliminary versions were published in 1586 and 1591, then widely distributed for comments and corrections[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]. After further interchange, commission meetings, and editorial work, the definitive Ratio Studiorum was published on January 8, 1599[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

The process of creating the Ratio Studiorum resulted in "a 'system' of schools whose strength and influence lay in the common spirit that evolved into common pedagogical principles. The pedagogy was based on experience, then refined and adapted through constant interchange. It was the first such educational system that the world had ever seen"[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

The system developed and expanded for more than two hundred years. By the early 18th century, the network of Jesuit educational institutions had become extensive, encompassing numerous schools throughout Europe and the Americas, with expansion into Asia and Africa[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

## Impact of the Suppression and Restoration

The suppression of the Society of Jesus in 1773 by papal order resulted in a catastrophic blow to Jesuit education. The network of 845 educational institutions spread throughout Europe and the Americas, Asia and Africa was largely destroyed[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]. Only a few Jesuit schools remained in Russian territories, where the suppression never took effect[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

When Pope Pius VII restored the Society of Jesus in 1814, one of the principal reasons he cited for this action was "so that the Catholic Church could have, once again, the benefit of their educational experience"[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]. Educational work began again almost immediately, and in 1832 an experimental revision of the Ratio Studiorum was published, though it was never definitively approved[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

The 19th century proved challenging for the restored Jesuit educational mission. The turmoil of the era, marked by revolutions and frequent expulsions of Jesuits from various countries and their schools, prevented any genuine renewal in Jesuit educational philosophy or pedagogy[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]. The Society itself was often divided, and its educational institutions were sometimes enlisted in ideological support of warring nations[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]. Nevertheless, in difficult situations and especially in the developing nations of the Americas, India, and East Asia, Jesuit schools began to flourish again[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

## Modern Era

The 20th century, particularly after the Second World War, brought dramatic increases in the size and number of Jesuit schools. Seeds of renewed spirit were planted through the decrees of various General Congregations, notably with applications of the Second Vatican Council incorporated into decree 28 of General Congregation XXXI[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

Today, the Jesuit educational apostolate extends to more than 2,000 educational institutions of diverse types and levels[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]. Ten thousand Jesuits work in close collaboration with nearly 100,000 lay people, providing education for more than 1,500,000 young people and adults in 56 countries around the world[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

Modern Jesuit education does not form the unified system of the 17th century. While many principles of the original Ratio remain valid, a uniform curriculum and structure imposed on all schools has been replaced by responsiveness to the distinct needs of different cultures and religious faiths, along with refined pedagogical methods that vary from culture to culture[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

However, a Jesuit "system" of education remains possible through the common spirit and vision of Ignatius. The basic goals, purposes, and policies derived from this common vision can apply to Jesuit schools today in all countries throughout the world, even when concrete applications differ significantly or when details of school life are determined by cultural factors or outside agencies[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af].

In the United States, twenty-seven Jesuit colleges and universities serve students across the country[^chunk:d4f703b8-e359-5cf3-a646-0073f5112c5f]. Jesuit education is characterized as "a Catholic educational tradition rooted in the spirituality of St. Ignatius Loyola and the Society of Jesus" that "combines rigorous inquiry with reflection, ethical discernment, service, and care for each student as a whole person"[^chunk:d4f703b8-e359-5cf3-a646-0073f5112c5f].
