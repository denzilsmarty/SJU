## Overview

The International Commission on the Apostolate of Jesuit Education (ICAJE) produced two landmark documents that together constitute the modern framework for Jesuit pedagogy: *The Characteristics of Jesuit Education* (1986) and *Ignatian Pedagogy: A Practical Approach* (1993). The 1986 document articulated the vision; the 1993 document answered educators who asked how that vision should shape ordinary teaching.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## The Characteristics of Jesuit Education (1986)

### Purpose and context

*The Characteristics of Jesuit Education* was issued by ICAJE in 1986 as a vision document for the global Jesuit school network. It articulated **28 characteristics** grouped into nine thematic sections, describing Jesuit education as world-affirming, person-centered, value-oriented, faith-filled, committed to justice, oriented to service, collaborative, and adaptive.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

A central organizing principle of the 1986 document is the concept of *human excellence*: the fullest development of each person's capacities for service, judged with attention to circumstances rather than by a single comparative standard. Academic excellence is located *within* this broader human excellence, not above it.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

### Key thematic areas of the 28 characteristics

Although the source documents do not enumerate each characteristic individually in the excerpts available here, the 1986 *Characteristics* document groups its 28 points into nine sections. The major themes documented in the authoritative secondary summary include:[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

- **World-affirming and person-centered**: education that respects and develops the whole person;
- **Value-oriented**: attention to moral formation and the integration of values with knowledge;
- **Faith-filled**: education offered within a faith horizon, while respecting the conscience of all;
- **Committed to justice**: the social consequences of learning and scholarship;
- **Oriented to service**: educating students for service of others and the common good;
- **Collaborative**: shared mission among Jesuits and lay colleagues;
- **Adaptive**: adjustment to regions, cultures, disciplines, and historical circumstances;
- **Whole-person care** (*cura personalis*): attention to student growth in all dimensions, including lifelong growth beyond formal education; and
- **Academic and co-curricular integration**: active student engagement as part of the educational design.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

The precise text of all 28 characteristics is contained in the promulgated ICAJE document; the summaries above reflect the groupings described in the authoritative interpretive background used here.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

### Relationship to the 1993 IPP

The 1993 *Ignatian Pedagogy* document was written explicitly as a **practical companion** to the 1986 *Characteristics*. Where the 1986 document described *what* Jesuit education should be, the 1993 document addressed *how* educators should bring that vision into teaching. Later, ICAJE's 2019 document *A Living Tradition* stated that it complements rather than replaces either the 1986 *Characteristics* or the 1993 IPP.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## Ignatian Pedagogy: A Practical Approach (1993)

### Origins and purpose

Promulgated in 1993 by ICAJE under Superior General Peter-Hans Kolvenbach, S.J., *Ignatian Pedagogy: A Practical Approach* (IPP) answered a practical question: given the vision of the 1986 *Characteristics*, how should educators actually teach? The document explicitly rejects the possibility of a single Jesuit curriculum for every country and discipline. Instead, it offers a flexible pattern—a **paradigm**—adaptable to different cultures, institutions, subjects, learners, and historical circumstances.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

The document invites educators to incorporate whatever sound methods best support integral intellectual, social, moral, and religious formation. It also makes **staff development central**: the paradigm requires practice, shared reflection, and in many cases a change of heart, not merely distribution of a document.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

### The five dynamics of the Ignatian Pedagogical Paradigm

The 1993 document defines five named dynamics. These are best visualized as a **recursive spiral** rather than a linear sequence: the elements interact and recur, and each cycle opens into the next.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

#### 1. Context

Context includes the learner's prior knowledge, experiences, feelings, values, readiness, social relationships, culture, and material conditions. It also encompasses the institutional setting and the wider economic, political, religious, technological, and cultural environment. The educator's own assumptions and location are part of the context.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

The 1993 text links context to *cura personalis*, making mutual trust and a personal teacher–student relationship central (IPP, nn. 33–41). Context is not a demographic preface completed once; it is an ongoing inquiry into what makes learning possible, difficult, meaningful, risky, or distorted.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

#### 2. Experience

Experience is any direct or vicarious encounter with the subject matter that the learner appropriates through the senses, intellect, imagination, memory, and affect. The IPP distinguishes cognitive grasp from affective response but insists that meaningful learning engages both (IPP, nn. 42–46).[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

The category is deliberately wide. Immediate forms of experience include laboratory, clinical, community, studio, performance, field, and interpersonal encounters. Mediated experience occurs through texts, data, testimony, cases, images, simulations, and historical imagination. A student's sustained encounter with a difficult theorem or poem counts as an Ignatian experience if it is intellectually real, personally appropriated, and open to reflection.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

> **Key distinction**: Exposure alone is not experience in the IPP sense. A trip, guest speaker, service day, or simulation may confirm prejudice if learners are unprepared.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

#### 3. Reflection

Reflection is the disciplined process by which learners grasp the significance of experience. It draws on memory, understanding, imagination, and feeling; examines causes, assumptions, motives, implications, alternatives, and consequences; and seeks greater freedom for choice. It is the **pivotal dynamic** connecting experience to responsible action (IPP, nn. 47–58).[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

Ignatian reflection is not synonymous with journaling, introspection, or stating an opinion. It is epistemic, affective, ethical, social, and—when appropriate—spiritual. The 1993 framework suggests reflection can move through five kinds of question:[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

1. **Attention**: What happened? What do the evidence and the perspectives actually show?
2. **Interpretation**: What patterns, causes, assumptions, or concepts explain it?
3. **Interior response**: What draws, troubles, resists, or energizes me? What may that response reveal or conceal?
4. **Ethical and social meaning**: Who benefits, who bears risk, whose voice is absent, and what consequences follow?
5. **Discernment**: Which possible response is more truthful, responsible, feasible, and conducive to human flourishing and the common good?[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

#### 4. Action

In the IPP, action begins in **interior appropriation**: a judgment, choice, priority, value, or commitment that follows reflection. It may then become outward conduct. Reflection is incomplete if it produces understanding without considering response; action is irresponsible if it bypasses reflection (IPP, nn. 59–62).[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

This twofold (interior and exterior) account is significant. A student who lacks the authority or opportunity to solve a structural problem during a course may still perform genuine action through a clarified professional commitment, a revised research design, a refusal of an unethical practice, or a plan for further inquiry. Conversely, visible activity without changed understanding may be pedagogically empty.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

Forms of action can include applying a concept to a novel problem; revising a design, argument, diagnosis, or performance; making a professional or vocational choice; serving with a community partner; advocating or participating civically; or committing to learn before taking further action.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

#### 5. Evaluation

The 1993 document affirms conventional assessment of academic mastery and asks educators to attend also to **broader human growth**. It suggests self-evaluation, mentoring, journals, and dialogue within relationships of trust. Evaluation provides feedback, uncovers blind spots, and **begins a new cycle** (IPP, nn. 63–70).[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

Evaluation therefore has at least four objects: (1) academic learning—what knowledge and skills have been demonstrated; (2) quality of judgment—how well evidence, values, perspectives, and consequences have been integrated; (3) action and impact—what was done and with what results; and (4) educational design—what the teacher, program, or institution should change.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

The 1993 document's formative ambition creates a serious risk: converting accompaniment into surveillance. Educators may assess the quality of ethical analysis, reflective practice, and professional responsibility, but should not claim to measure the state of a student's conscience, compassion, faith, or political virtue.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## The Learner as a Whole, Relational Person

A foundational anthropological commitment runs beneath all five dynamics. The IPP assumes that a **learner is more than a processor of information**. Learning involves intellect, memory, imagination, emotion, embodiment, relationships, social location, desire, and will.[^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0]

Persons are shaped by culture and institutions but retain **agency**. They can grow in freedom: the capacity to perceive reality, recognize attachments and pressures, deliberate, and choose what is more truthful, loving, just, and conducive to the common good. This is why the IPP resists both content-only instruction and manipulation.[^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0]

The teacher does not simply affirm whatever a learner already believes, nor does the teacher stand between the learner and reality as its sole interpreter. The educator designs conditions in which the learner can encounter a subject, evidence, other persons, and consequences with increasing honesty and skill. Authority is exercised through expertise, clarity, care, feedback, and appropriate challenge.[^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0]

This relational account of the learner means that:

- **Formation is the horizon**, not production of compliant students. Formation is intentional, accompanied, and lifelong integration of knowledge, experience, reflection, relationships, faith or ultimate meaning, and responsible action.[^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0]
- ***Cura personalis* is a relational commitment** requiring knowledge of learners, trust, appropriate challenge, and adaptation—not a sixth IPP step and not simply "being nice."[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]
- **Pluralism is compatible with fidelity.** The 1993 document describes the paradigm as faith-inspired and profoundly human, accessible to people who do not share Christian belief.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] [^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0]
- **Freedom of conscience is non-negotiable.** A teacher may disclose commitments and invite ethical examination; a teacher may not manufacture assent.[^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0]

The 1993 document's vision of the whole person also grounds its insistence that experience engage both *cognitive grasp* and *affective response*—neither can be dispensed with if learning is to be appropriated and transformative rather than merely transmitted.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## The Paradigm as Recursive Architecture

The five dynamics should be visualized as a **spiral**, not a line. New context can change the meaning of an experience; reflection can reveal that more experience is needed; evaluation can become the experience for a new cycle; action can expose assumptions that require reconsideration.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

This recursiveness is what the 1993 document means by calling the IPP a *paradigm*—an adaptable pattern that orders judgment without prescribing a single sequence or a universal curriculum.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

| Dynamic | Governing question | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Context | Who is learning what, where, under which conditions and histories? | Treating learners as interchangeable |
| Experience | What reality will the learner encounter and appropriate? | Confusing activity or exposure with learning |
| Reflection | What does this mean, and what is being asked of us? | Opinion-sharing without disciplined inquiry, or steering to a preferred answer |
| Action | What interior choice or outward response follows? | Activity without discernment, or insight without response |
| Evaluation | What was learned, what changed, and what should happen next? | Reducing evaluation to grades or grading moral worth |[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]
