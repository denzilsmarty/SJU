## What is the Ratio Studiorum?

The Ratio Studiorum, or "Plan of Studies," is a handbook that assisted teachers and administrators in the daily operation of Jesuit schools.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] In its final form, it is a series of practical directives regarding the government of the school, the formation and distribution of teachers, the curriculum, and methods of teaching.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] Like Part IV of the Constitutions, it is not an original work but rather a collection of the most effective educational methods of the time, tested and adapted for the purposes of Jesuit schools.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

### Development and Publication Timeline

The Ratio Studiorum emerged from a practical necessity. Following the death of Ignatius of Loyola in 1556, Jesuit involvement in education continued to grow rapidly. Of the 40 schools that Ignatius had personally approved, at least 35 were in operation when he died, even though the total membership of the Society had not yet reached 1,000. Within forty years, the number of Jesuit schools would reach 245.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

Successive Jesuit superiors encouraged an exchange of ideas based on concrete experiences so that, without violating the Ignatian principle that "circumstances of place and persons" be taken into account, a basic curriculum and pedagogy could be developed which would be common to all Jesuit schools.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] The first drafts of this common document were based on the "Rules of the Roman College," which Ignatius himself had established in 1551 as a model for all Jesuit schools throughout the world.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

An international committee of six Jesuits was appointed by Superior General Claudio Acquaviva to adapt and modify these tentative drafts on the basis of experiences in other parts of the world.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] In 1586 and again in 1591, this group published more comprehensive drafts which were widely distributed for comments and corrections. Further interchange, commission meetings, and editorial work resulted, finally, in the publication of a definitive Ratio Studiorum on January 8, 1599.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

## How the Ratio Studiorum Structures Jesuit Education and Pedagogy

### Underlying Principles from Ignatian Spirituality

Although the final 1599 edition of the Ratio contains little explicit reference to underlying principles flowing from Ignatius' experience and his Companions, these principles had been stated in earlier versions and were presupposed in the final edition.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] The spirit of the Ratio was the vision of Ignatius, even though this was not stated explicitly.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

The relationship between teacher and student was to be modeled on the relationship between the director of the Spiritual Exercises and the person making them.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] The "Presupposition" to the Exercises—which requires that one put a good interpretation on another's statement—became the norm for establishing personal relations and good rapport throughout the educational community.[^chunk:9e735b68-185c-5424-b033-827b29c9b312]

### Pedagogical Methods

The pedagogy included several interconnected methods derived from the Spiritual Exercises:[^chunk:9e735b68-185c-5424-b033-827b29c9b312]

- The "preludes" and "points" for prayer in the Exercises corresponded to the prelection, or preliminary presentation, of course material to be covered
- The "repetition" of prayer became the mastery of course material through frequent and careful repetition of class work
- The "application of the senses" (what Ignatius called "sentir") was found in the stress on the creative and the imaginative, in the stress on experience, motivation, appreciation and joy in learning[^chunk:9e735b68-185c-5424-b033-827b29c9b312]

The active role of the person making the Exercises was the model for the active role of the student in personal study, personal discovery, and creativity.[^chunk:9e735b68-185c-5424-b033-827b29c9b312]

The progression in the Exercises was one source of the practical, disciplined, "means to end" approach that is characteristic of Jesuit education.[^chunk:9e735b68-185c-5424-b033-827b29c9b312]

### Curricular Organization

Part IV of the Ignatius' Constitutions established priorities that became priorities of Jesuit education: a stress on the humanities, to be followed by philosophy and theology, a careful orderly advance to be observed in pursuing these successive branches of knowledge, repetition of the material and active involvement of the students in their own education.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] Much time should be spent in developing good style in writing.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

The role of the Rector, as the center of authority, inspiration, and unity, was essential.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

The curriculum was to be structured carefully: in daily order, in the way that courses build on material covered in previous courses, and in the way courses are related to one another.[^chunk:9e735b68-185c-5424-b033-827b29c9b312] The curriculum should be so integrated that each individual course contributes toward the overall goal of the school.[^chunk:9e735b68-185c-5424-b033-827b29c9b312]

The pedagogy was to include analysis, repetition, active reflection, and synthesis; it should combine theoretical ideas with their applications.[^chunk:9e735b68-185c-5424-b033-827b29c9b312] It was not the quantity of course material covered that was important but rather a solid, profound, and basic formation—expressed in the Latin maxim "Non multa, sed multum" (not many things, but much).[^chunk:9e735b68-185c-5424-b033-827b29c9b312]

## Subjects and Disciplines Emphasized in the Original Ratio Studiorum

If we look to the foundational documents that informed the Ratio Studiorum, Part IV of the Constitutions of the Society of Jesus specified the academic program. The preamble to Part IV states that "The aim which the Society of Jesus directly seeks is to aid its own members and their fellowmen to attain the ultimate end for which they were created. To achieve this purpose, in addition to the example of one's life, learning and a method of expounding it are also necessary."[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

### Primary Emphasis: Theology

Since the end of the Society and of its studies was to aid fellow men to the knowledge and love of God and to the salvation of their souls, theology was the principal emphasis in Jesuit universities and colleges, with diligent treatment by highly capable professors given to scholastic doctrine and Sacred Scripture.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23]

### Essential Preparation: Humane Letters and Languages

Since both the learning of theology and the use of it required, especially in the sixteenth century, knowledge of humane letters and of the Latin and Greek languages, there were to be capable professors of these languages in sufficient number.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23] Under the heading of humane letters was understood, in addition to grammar, what pertained to rhetoric, poetry, and history.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23]

There could also be teachers of other languages such as Hebrew, Chaldaic, Arabic, and Indian when these would be necessary or useful for the work of the Society.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23] When a college planned to prepare persons to work among the Moors or Turks, Arabic or Chaldaic would be suitable; Indian would be for those destined for work among the Indians.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23]

### Philosophy and Natural Sciences

Since the arts or natural sciences prepare the intellectual powers for theology and are highly serviceable for the perfect understanding and use of it, they were to be treated with fitting diligence and by learned teachers.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23] Logic, physics, metaphysics, and moral philosophy were to be treated, along with mathematics with moderation appropriate to secure the end sought.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23]

### Fields Not Pursued

The study of medicine and laws, being rather remote from the Jesuit Institute, were not to be taught in the universities of the Society, or at least the Society would not undertake this activity through its own members.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23]

### Methods of Teaching the Branches

For the treatment both of the lower branches and of theology, there was to be a suitable order and arrangement both for the morning and the afternoon.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23] Although the order and hours devoted to these studies might vary according to the regions and times of the year, there was to be such conformity that in every region a procedure was used which was judged most expedient for greater progress in learning.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23]

There were to be not only public lectures but also different masters according to the capacity and number of students.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23] These masters were to take an interest in the progress of each student, require them to give an account of their lessons and to repeat them, make those studying the humanities get practice by ordinarily speaking Latin, by composing, and by delivering well what they had composed, and make them engage frequently in disputations.[^chunk:375a57ee-3cd7-523a-b4cc-cda9b3166f23]

## A System of Schools

The process leading to and resulting in the publication of the Ratio produced a "system" of schools whose strength and influence lay in the common spirit that evolved into common pedagogical principles.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] The pedagogy was based on experience, then refined and adapted through constant interchange.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] It was the first such educational system that the world had ever seen.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

The system of Jesuit schools developed and expanded for more than two hundred years, and then came to a sudden end when the Society of Jesus was suppressed by Papal Order in 1773, destroying a network of 845 educational institutions spread throughout Europe and the Americas, Asia and Africa.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] Only a few Jesuit schools remained in Russian territories, where the suppression never took effect.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

When Pius VII restored the Society of Jesus in 1814, one of the reasons he gave was "so that the Catholic Church could have, once again, the benefit of their educational experience."[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] Educational work began again almost immediately, and in 1832, an experimental revision of the Ratio Studiorum was published, though it was never definitively approved.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]

Today, the Jesuit educational apostolate extends to more than 2,000 educational institutions of a bewildering variety of types and levels, with 10,000 Jesuits working in close collaboration with nearly 100,000 lay people, providing education for more than 1,500,000 young people and adults in 56 countries around the world.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af] Although Jesuit education today does not form the unified system of the 17th Century, and though a uniform curriculum imposed on all schools throughout the world has been replaced by the distinct needs of different cultures and religious faiths, the common spirit that enabled the first Jesuit schools to evolve common principles remains possible.[^chunk:80b71a55-5eea-539d-842b-092523aa22af]
