Peter-Hans Kolvenbach, S.J., Superior General of the Society of Jesus from 1983 to 2008, delivered two foundational addresses on Jesuit higher education that together constitute the most cited modern account of the university's purpose and the standard by which it should be judged. The 2000 address at Santa Clara University and the 2001 address on the Ignatian charism are authoritative Tier 3 sources in the Jesuit educational tradition and are essential reading for anyone working within that tradition.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## The 2000 Santa Clara Address: Who Students Become

Kolvenbach's 2000 address, formally titled *The Service of Faith and the Promotion of Justice in American Jesuit Higher Education*, was delivered at Santa Clara University and quickly became a touchstone for Jesuit higher education in the United States.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

The address's central and most-quoted argument is a shift in the standard of evaluation for a Jesuit university. Kolvenbach argued that the proper measure of such an institution is not what students know when they leave, but **who they become**.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] This formulation reframes institutional accountability: academic excellence, disciplinary mastery, and professional preparation are necessary but not sufficient. The deeper question is whether the educational process forms persons whose learning issues in solidarity, responsibility, and care for the common good.

Kolvenbach further argued that this kind of **educated solidarity** does not arise from intellectual formation alone. It normally requires **contact with the suffering of others**, joined to sustained intellectual inquiry and moral reflection.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] Mere exposure is insufficient; what is required is the combination of direct or proximate encounter, disciplined analysis, and personal appropriation through reflection. This three-part movement—contact, inquiry, reflection—maps closely onto the dynamics of what would later be articulated in the Ignatian Pedagogical Paradigm as experience, reflection, and action, though the Santa Clara address predates neither the IPP nor the broader tradition from which both draw.
