## Core Principles of Ignatian Pedagogy

Ignatian pedagogy is grounded in the spiritual vision of Saint Ignatius of Loyola and operates through a distinctive set of principles and practices. The foundational framework organizes Jesuit education across six interconnected axes: the canonical source documents, anchor concepts and vocabulary, pedagogical methods, curricular architecture, formation outcomes, and strategic horizons.[^chunk:ee9edb86-4abc-5e21-9216-74016824bd16]

At its heart, Ignatian pedagogy rests on the **Ignatian Pedagogical Paradigm (IPP)**, a five-stage cycle that structures learning and formation:[^chunk:1ec06229-98d0-597a-b622-069fc2aac210] context, experience, reflection, action, and evaluation. This approach emerged from the *Ignatian Pedagogy: A Practical Approach* document (1993), which translates the principles of the Spiritual Exercises into a practical teaching methodology.[^chunk:1ec06229-98d0-597a-b622-069fc2aac210] Teachers are invited to possess an astute understanding of the context in which teaching takes place; create experiences that stretch the intellect and imagination of students; foster an ongoing habit of reflection to go deeper into any experience; provide opportunities to put the values and skills acquired into action in service to the Gospel; and engage in rich, multifaceted assessments of teaching and learning.[^chunk:1ec06229-98d0-597a-b622-069fc2aac210]

The pedagogical method operates across multiple scales. The **Examen**—a daily reflective practice—functions at the daily level.[^chunk:f8970d96-7ea3-5963-b5d6-9c9d248a8614] The IPP operates at the course or experience scale, while the Spiritual Exercises themselves provide a pedagogy of imaginative contemplation, colloquy, and election that operates at retreat or extended-formation scale.[^chunk:ee9edb86-4abc-5e21-9216-74016824bd16] Few traditions in any civilization have an equivalently structured layered method.[^chunk:ee9edb86-4abc-5e21-9216-74016824bd16]

## Cura Personalis: The Care of the Individual

**Cura personalis**—the care of the individual—is a foundational principle that shapes how Jesuit educators approach their work.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307] Grounded in the principle of cura personalis, Jesuit schools must value the dignity and worth of each member of their community.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

In contemporary practice, cura personalis manifests in several ways. Jesuit schools should be excellent in humanizing their communities, helping students and staff to deepen their empathy and their lasting bonds of friendship.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307] Schools must also teach about the dignity of women, who are full and equal partners in all endeavors; actively combat racism in all of its forms; and create a climate where all God's children, regardless of their sexual orientation, are welcome.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

The practice of cura personalis is supported by the spiritual-direction and accompaniment tradition, which is an explicit 475-year-old Ignatian methodology with no secular equivalent of comparable depth.[^chunk:f8970d96-7ea3-5963-b5d6-9c9d248a8614]

## The Magis: The Greater Good

**Magis**—a Latin term meaning "the greater" or "more"—names the Ignatian orientation toward the qualitatively greater good.[^chunk:ee9edb86-4abc-5e21-9216-74016824bd16] This principle challenges educators and students to continually ask what more can be done, what deeper engagement is possible, and how excellence can be pursued not merely for its own sake but in service to the greater glory of God and the common good.

The concept of magis operates across Jesuit educational practice as a driver of continuous improvement and discernment. It is inseparable from AMDG (*Ad Maiorem Dei Gloriam*—for the greater glory of God), which names the orientational principle to which all Jesuit activity is ordered.[^chunk:ee9edb86-4abc-5e21-9216-74016824bd16] Both concepts work together to invite educators and students to stretch beyond what is merely adequate or comfortable, to seek the deeper good that serves God's purposes in the world.

## Finding God in All Things

**Finding God in all things** is the foundational Ignatian disposition that authorizes much of Jesuit educational practice.[^chunk:f8970d96-7ea3-5963-b5d6-9c9d248a8614] This means experiencing the generative, expansive core of wonder, hope, joy, delight, compassion, and connection everywhere, with all, in all.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

As educators, the task is to refresh and deepen our own spirituality so that we are able to model for students an engagement with the world characterized by intense curiosity, creativity, delight in discovery, confidence, connection, and compassion for all that exists.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307] The famous quote attributed to Father Pedro Arrupe captures the existential import: "Nothing is more practical than finding God. It is falling in Love in a quite absolute, final way. What you are in love with, what seizes your imagination, will affect everything."[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

Jesuit schools must offer opportunities, in and out of the classroom, for students to experience the world in this contemplative way and to fall in love with it.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307] Schools must offer opportunities that develop the reflective spirituality that continually seeks to find God, a spirituality that has the strength to grow as students mature and can accompany them for the rest of their lives.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

## Human Excellence: Conscience, Competence, Compassion, and Commitment

Contemporary Jesuit education aims to develop women and men of **conscience, compassion, commitment, and competence**—the modern articulation of human excellence.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307] This framework emerged from foundational letters by Father Kolvenbach, who articulated the goal of Jesuit educators as forming "men and women of competence, conscience, and compassionate commitment."[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

These four dimensions work together:

- **Conscience**: Students develop knowledge of themselves through cultivating a spiritual life, and gain consistent knowledge and experience of society and its imbalances.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

- **Compassion**: Students develop the ability to open their hearts to be in solidarity with and assume the suffering of others.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

- **Commitment**: Being compassionate, students honestly strive toward faith and, through peaceful means, work for social and political transformation of their countries and social structures to achieve justice.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

- **Competence**: Students must be capable of creating, understanding and using knowledge and skills to live in and transform their own context; able to be part of the changing and diverse world creating a life project for others and with others; and able to develop the intellectual, academic, emotional, and social skills required for professional and human achievement.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

The traditional emphasis on academic excellence is not neglected but rather framed within this broader context of human excellence, allowing Jesuit schools to fulfill their fundamental social role and enter into dialogue with the larger society about the meaning of quality education.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

## Lifelong Learning and Discernment

A core principle of Jesuit education is commitment to lifelong learning rooted in the practice of **discernment**.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307] Learning to listen to and accept the unique gifts and talents, questions and anxieties, great joys and deep desires is the ultimate gift of education rooted in an incarnational worldview.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

The questions that Jesuit education invites students to ask throughout their lives include: How will I spend my life? To whom do I belong? Where is God calling me? Contemplating the regard of the Holy Trinity on our world, do our graduates ask: How can I make use of my God-given gifts and talents to respond to the needs of people in my family, my locality, my region of the world, and the global community?[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]

These questions have always been the questions beneath the questions in Jesuit classrooms. Consequently, the ultimate success of Jesuit educational endeavor cannot be measured by who the graduate is at the moment of graduation. Instead, the gift of Jesuit schooling is best measured by how graduates engage life in the decades after graduation—whether the partly answered questions sparked in the classroom continue to be asked and re-asked throughout one's life, and whether an encounter with Christ through the Spirit makes a difference in how the graduate discerns questions of career, lifestyle, values, and measures of success or failure.[^chunk:9de43838-db1d-5aba-806c-c95f66998307]
