# Sample IPP Learning Cycle: Allocating a Scarce Health Resource

This article walks through a complete, concrete example of the Ignatian Pedagogical Paradigm (IPP) in practice, using a case drawn from health professions and nursing education. The example is drawn directly from the IPP source-of-truth guide developed for Saint Joseph's University and is meant to illustrate how the five dynamics—context, experience, reflection, action, and evaluation—work together in a single, coherent learning arc.[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940] The same architecture can be adapted across disciplines, as noted below.

---

## Why This Case?

Health professions and nursing education offers a natural home for IPP design because clinical practice already cycles through context, encounter, reflection, action, and review.[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940] The allocation of a scarce health resource is an especially rich topic because it is technically demanding, ethically contested, emotionally weighted, and structurally revealing—making each of the five dynamics both necessary and visible.

---

## The Five Dynamics in the Health Resource Case

### 1. Context

> *Governing question: Who is learning what, where, under which conditions and histories?*

In the IPP, context is not a demographic preface completed once. It is an ongoing inquiry into what makes learning possible, difficult, meaningful, risky, or distorted.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] For the scarce-resource case, this means students examine:

- the health system and its legal rules;
- histories of exclusion from care;
- institutional constraints on resource supply;
- and their own prior assumptions about who deserves care and why.[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

In health professions education more broadly, context includes the patient's life, social determinants of health, team dynamics, the available evidence base, regulatory requirements, and the power dynamics of clinical environments.[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

Faculty attending to context ask: What do learners already know, misunderstand, fear, value, or hope? What identities and prior experiences are salient? What histories and power relations shape the subject?[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

---

### 2. Experience

> *Governing question: What reality will the learner encounter and appropriate?*

Experience in the IPP sense is any direct or vicarious encounter with the subject matter that the learner appropriates through the senses, intellect, imagination, memory, and affect—engaging both cognitive grasp and affective response.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300] In the health resource case:

- Teams analyze a **realistic case**, working with real data about scarcity (e.g., ventilator or organ supply).
- Students engage **patient narratives** that give human faces to statistical abstractions.
- Students examine the **roles and perspectives of clinicians, administrators, and families**, each carrying different information, authority, and moral stakes.[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

Strong experience is aligned to a significant learning purpose; authentic enough to reveal the complexity of the subject; accessible and safe enough for meaningful participation; and structured so that learners must notice rather than merely attend.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

A key guardrail: exposure alone is not experience in the IPP sense. A simulation or case study may confirm prejudice if learners are unprepared. Emotional intensity is not evidence of depth.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

---

### 3. Reflection

> *Governing question: What does this mean, why does it matter, and what is being asked of us?*

Reflection is the pivotal dynamic connecting experience to responsible action. It is the disciplined process by which learners grasp the significance of experience, drawing on memory, understanding, imagination, and feeling; examining causes, assumptions, motives, implications, alternatives, and consequences; and seeking greater freedom for choice.[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

In the health resource case, reflection specifically requires students to:

- **compare ethical frameworks** (e.g., utilitarian maximization of lives saved vs. egalitarian lottery vs. first-come-first-served vs. prognosis-based triage);
- **identify affective responses and biases**—what draws, troubles, resists, or energizes them, and what that may reveal or conceal;
- **test assumptions** about whose life counts more and why;
- and **listen to perspectives with unequal power**, including patients who have historically been excluded.[^chunk:a6c62be7-4932-5dfc-b4ea-52911d9de940]

#### The Disciplined Reflection Sequence

The IPP describes reflection as moving through five kinds of question:[^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

| Step | Question |
|---|---|
| **Attention** | What happened? What do the evidence and the perspectives actually show? |
| **Interpretation** | What patterns, causes, assumptions, or concepts explain it? |
| **Interior response** | What draws, troubles, resists, or energizes me? What may that reveal or conceal? |
| **Ethical and social meaning** | Who benefits, who bears risk, whose voice is absent, and what consequences follow? |
| **Discernment** | Which possible response is more truthful, responsible, feasible, and conducive to human flourishing? |

#### Integrating Ethical Frameworks

Applying ethical frameworks is not a substitute for reflection—it is its intellectual core.
