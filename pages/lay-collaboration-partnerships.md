## Evolution of Jesuit-Lay Collaboration

The relationship between Jesuits and lay educators has undergone significant transformation over the past several decades. Historically, lay participation in Jesuit institutions was minimal and narrowly defined. In the 16th-century Constitutions, the only formal role assigned to lay people in Jesuit education was that of "corrector"—a disciplinary function.[^chunk:71347df1-4b49-537d-aabd-99715fae0d7a] This limited engagement reflected the Society's initial conception of its educational mission.

The shift toward meaningful lay collaboration emerged gradually. A major turning point came with the General Congregation's two Decrees adopted just over six years prior to 2001: "Cooperation with the Laity in Mission" and "Jesuits and the Situation of Women in Church and Civil Society."[^chunk:71347df1-4b49-537d-aabd-99715fae0d7a] These documents were considered innovative at the time, though implementation varied across institutions. The numerical reality drove this evolution: the proportion of lay educators to Jesuits in Jesuit educational work reached approximately 95% lay and 5% Jesuits.[^chunk:71347df1-4b49-537d-aabd-99715fae0d7a]

By the 2018 World Assembly of Jesuit Universities, collaboration had become explicitly framed as a defining characteristic of Jesuit identity itself. The General Congregations following the Second Vatican Council defined collaboration as part of Jesuit identity, with the Society understanding itself as "this least and collaborative Society" working together with forces greater than itself.[^chunk:4a09e55b-ed31-537f-a4de-6bba99c130f4]

## Challenges and Opportunities for Maintaining Jesuit Identity

Jesuit-lay collaboration presents both significant challenges and substantial opportunities for preserving institutional identity. The identity question is particularly acute: "The identity of Jesuit higher education is at stake for the short term, especially in the West and in the industrialized countries."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03] The "problem of the 'next generation'" is not theoretical—as the physical presence of Jesuits diminishes, the risk that "the ethos of the institution, its Ignatian, Catholic, Christian culture, may also disappear" becomes concrete.[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

A critical insight is that different levels of collaboration are appropriate for different persons and vocations. "Not all collaboration with the laity is in keeping with the mission." While Jesuits can be assumed to identify with their mission, "we cannot assume that all the lay people identify themselves with the specific mission of the Jesuits. Lay people are not called to be mini-Jesuits, but rather to live their own lay vocation."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

The Society emphasizes that discriminating among personnel based on supposed commitment levels would be counterproductive. Instead, drawing on Ignatian spirituality's principle that "there is no worse error in spiritual life than trying to lead all by the same road," the Society proposes rather than imposes its mission. "In an 'interface' of mutual respect and sincerity, collaborators are invited to share this mission and make it their own, to different degrees."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

### Concrete Practices for Deepening Shared Mission

Several evidence-based practices help maintain Jesuit identity while deepening lay collaboration:

**Orientation and Formation Programs**: Courses for new professors and board members share the institution's ethos. While not all lay persons will commit fully to the Jesuit mission, "the Society expects of all, including people of other faiths, that they recognize and accept the values contained in the Ignatian spirituality and apostolic mission that animate the work."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

**Ongoing Formation**: Programs for both lay people and Jesuits aim to "form an apostolic team of Jesuits and colleagues for the purpose of realizing the Jesuit identity and mission of the work." This approach seeks to create the "critical mass" necessary to ensure institutional identity.[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

**Mission-Aligned Hiring**: "Hiring for mission" is described as delicate but legitimate. While universities cannot discriminate illegally in personnel decisions, they have the right to "choose men and women capable of sharing its identity" when possible—a practice secular corporations regularly employ for their own aims.[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

**Spiritual Formation**: The Society offers the Spiritual Exercises to personnel in various formats, particularly through daily-life practice of the Exercises.[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

**Jesuit Presence and Influence**: "Even while responsibilities are shared more and more, or are transferred to non-Jesuit collaborators, the Jesuits, both as a community and as individuals, should see ways of still being present, now no longer exercising power but still exercising influence in the institution."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

The underlying principle reflects Ignatian wisdom: respecting how God leads each person while ensuring minimum standards of institutional coherence. "Preparation in the vision and the shared mission between Jesuits and collaborators is a priority of the first order in our higher education."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

## Building Networks for Collective Impact

Jesuit universities increasingly recognize that shared mission extends beyond individual institutions. As one leader noted, "We have a long way to go when it comes to incorporating this collaborative way of proceeding deeply in each of our universities. We also need our universities to collaborate with each other more, and more deeply, and also with other institutions, projects and groups that share goals with us."[^chunk:4a09e55b-ed31-537f-a4de-6bba99c130f4]

### Regional and International Coordination

By definition, universities possess "universality and the possibility of exchanges at all levels."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03] However, universities are typically "extremely jealous of their autonomy and independence, and more easily lend themselves to scientific exchange than to concrete forms of joint cooperation among equals."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

The need for coordination—often driven more by practical necessity than idealistic universalism—has led Jesuit higher education to organize regionally. Regional associations, while limited to servicing their members, are "absolutely indispensable if we hope to see the Society act as a body."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03] By 2001, Europe was planning to form its first association, which would include the Near East and Africa.[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

### Disciplinary and Specialized Networks

Beyond regional structures, numerous groups and platforms exist for scholarly encounters: "theology, philosophy, spirituality, social sciences, positive sciences, communication, research centers, journals, and surely many others. All of these accomplish their role in the universal apostolic service of the Society."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03] By its universal vocation and particularly in an age of globalization, the Society actively encourages these national and international networks.[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

### Consortium Models and International Cooperation

Networks enable Jesuit higher education to address global problems "by means of mutual assistance, information, planning and shared evaluation, or the putting into action of projects which are beyond the capacity of each individual institution."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

Successful examples demonstrate this potential:

- The MBA Program in Beijing under AJCU (Association of Jesuit Colleges and Universities) responsibility
- The consortium effort at The Beijing Center for Language and Culture
- Collaboration among various AJCU-EAO (East Asia and Oceania) universities in training professors in Cambodia and reconstructing the University of East Timor
- Coordination between AJCU and AUSJAL (Latin American association) with exchanges between Latin American universities and institutions in Spain and the United States
- Distance education programs with their substantial potential for mutual exchange[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

### The Ignatian Vision and Global Cooperation

The Ignatian principle of MAGIS—seeking "more universal" service—impels institutions beyond particularity. "Although each university has a particular responsibility in a concrete and limited place in the vineyard of the Lord, the Ignatian MAGIS and the 'more universal' impel us not to enclose ourselves in this particularity but to open out to a greater service in the Lord's vineyard."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

This vision particularly applies to developing countries: "As we consider more deeply the international dimension of the Society, it becomes clearer how much more we can accomplish by cooperation, not competition, as we venture abroad. This is especially true in developing countries." Consortium efforts have potential to reach Vietnam, Laos, East Timor, Cambodia, Africa, and developing countries worldwide.[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

Beyond institutional cooperation, such gatherings create space for "fraternal collaboration and concrete gestures of solidarity" between Jesuits and lay people from different continents. "The important thing is to cooperate together for the sake of our brothers and sisters around the world as we seek to put a human face on the process of globalization."[^chunk:9cd06de5-b134-5497-82b6-f7dcc671cd03]

### The International Association of Jesuit Universities

In 2018, Jesuit universities took a significant structural step toward enhanced collaboration. At the World Assembly, participants established the International Association of Jesuit Universities (IAJU), described as "beginning a new era and a new style of practical work in our universities."[^chunk:4a09e55b-ed31-537f-a4de-6bba99c130f4]

This development reflected explicit recognition that "collaboration will be more effective when we create networks between our schools. Overcoming the tendency to shepherd and strengthen resources for the use of each individual university alone will require a culture of generosity which is at the heart of doing more and greater things—and of a life lived in abundance."[^chunk:4a09e55b-ed31-537f-a4de-6bba99c130f4]

The assembly's work proceeded on the conviction that "the path that involves discernment, apostolic planning, collaboration, and trans-institutional networks" represents the way forward for Jesuit higher education.[^chunk:4a09e55b-ed31-537f-a4de-6bba99c130f4]

## Conclusion

Lay collaboration and institutional partnerships have evolved from minimal historical roles to central features of Jesuit higher education identity. This transformation requires intentional practices that simultaneously preserve Ignatian charism and respect lay persons' own vocations. Through regional associations, disciplinary networks, and consortium models—culminating in the establishment of the International Association of Jesuit Universities—Jesuit institutions are building capacity for collective impact aligned with their shared mission of serving faith, justice, and the common good.
