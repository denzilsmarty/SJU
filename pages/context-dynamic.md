# Context: The First Dynamic of the IPP

Context is the first of five dynamics in the Ignatian Pedagogical Paradigm (IPP) and the one that surrounds every other. Rather than a preliminary formality or a one-time demographic snapshot, context is an ongoing inquiry into the concrete conditions that make learning possible, meaningful, difficult, or distorted. This article explains what context means within the IPP, why it extends well beyond basic learner data, what categories of information educators should investigate, how institutional conditions belong inside context analysis, and how sustained attention to context differs from a single needs assessment conducted at the start of a term.

---

## 1. The Authoritative Definition

The 1993 ICAJE document *Ignatian Pedagogy: A Practical Approach* is the controlling source for the paradigm. It defines context to include the learner's **prior knowledge, experiences, feelings, values, readiness, social relationships, culture, and material conditions**, as well as the **institutional setting** and the wider **economic, political, religious, technological, and cultural environment**. The educator's own assumptions and social location are also part of the context. The document makes mutual trust and a personal teacher–student relationship central to context, linking it directly to *cura personalis*. [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

The governing question for this dynamic is: *Who is learning what, where, under which conditions and histories?* The primary educational work is to diagnose readiness, identity, prior knowledge, culture, power, purpose, and environment. The characteristic failure is treating learners as interchangeable. [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## 2. Why Context Is More Than a Demographic Snapshot

The IPP's vision of the learner is explicitly holistic. The paradigm assumes that a learner is **more than a processor of information**: learning involves intellect, memory, imagination, emotion, embodiment, relationships, social location, desire, and will. Persons are shaped by culture and institutions but retain agency. [^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0] A demographic snapshot—age, major, hometown—captures almost none of this depth.

Context, by contrast, encompasses several interlocking layers:

- **The individual learner**: what they already know, misunderstand, fear, value, or hope for in this particular subject
- **Relational and social conditions**: the identities and relationships that are salient in the classroom and the histories of power that shape them
- **Disciplinary history**: what the field counts as evidence, who has historically been authorized to produce knowledge, and what assumptions are embedded in the curriculum
- **Institutional conditions**: policies, technologies, schedules, and incentives that structure learning before any class meeting begins
- **The educator's own location**: the teacher's relationship to the subject and to the learners—their assumptions, privileges, and blind spots
- **The wider environment**: economic, political, religious, technological, and cultural forces acting on learners and shaping what knowledge means and costs [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

Because context operates at all these levels simultaneously, reducing it to a registration-data profile misses most of what the educator needs to design humane, rigorous, and appropriate learning encounters.

## 3. Prior Knowledge, Values, and Cultural Factors

The 1993 source document specifies several concrete questions educators should investigate. Translated for higher education, they include: [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

- What do learners already know, misunderstand, fear, value, or hope?
- What linguistic, cultural, financial, disability-related, family, work, or caregiving conditions affect participation?
- What identities and experiences are salient, and which should not be presumed?
- What histories and power relations shape the subject and the classroom?
- What does the discipline count as evidence, and who has historically been authorized to produce it?
- What is the educator's own relationship to the subject and to the learners?
- What support, accessibility, consent, and safeguarding conditions are necessary?

### 3.1 Prior Knowledge and Readiness

Readiness is not a fixed trait but a relational condition: a learner may be advanced in one dimension of a subject and a novice in another. Context analysis asks whether foundational knowledge is in place, where predictable misconceptions arise, and what intellectual or emotional distance learners bring to the material. Useful practices include **prior-knowledge probes and low-stakes diagnostic work**. [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

### 3.2 Values and Motivations

What learners care about—and what they fear—shapes how they receive experience and engage in reflection. Educators attentive to context ask not only whether students understand the content but whether they perceive it as meaningful, threatening, or irrelevant to anything they value. This is why context is linked in the 1993 document directly to *cura personalis*: personal knowledge of the learner is a precondition for genuine accompaniment. [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

### 3.3 Cultural Factors and Social Location

Culture, language, and social location are not decorative diversity considerations; they determine the terms on which a learner can enter a subject, whether they see themselves reflected in it, and what forms of engagement are accessible or alienating. Context analysis should include attention to **cultural history, language background, and the power relations that have shaped access to the discipline**. In professional programs, this extends to the moral ecology of practice: the norms, hierarchies, and exclusions that characterize the field learners are entering. [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

Context work becomes problematic, however, when it slides into **stereotyping**: inferring beliefs or capacity from group identity rather than attending to the actual person. The proper standard is **informed adaptation with high expectations and appropriate support**. [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

## 4. Institutional Conditions as Part of Context

The IPP makes a claim that many pedagogical frameworks omit: **the institution teaches**. Policies, incentives, technologies, spaces, workloads, partnerships, and decision processes form people as surely as syllabi do. An IPP-informed institutional question is what a university's arrangements cause people to experience, value, and do. [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

For course-level context analysis, this means educators should ask: [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

- What institutional policies, technologies, schedules, and incentives structure the learning experience?
- What accessibility requirements apply, and are they met before the course begins?
- What support services exist, and do students know how to reach them?
- What community or partnership conditions shape field or clinical experience?

For professional programs specifically, context includes **licensure standards, organizational constraints, community needs, and the moral ecology of practice**. In online learning environments, context includes **time zones, bandwidth, digital literacy, surveillance, platform design, and the conditions under which students are using AI tools**. [^chunk:9a34ed4d-6e02-5417-886f-cc974e519300]

At the institutional level, the IPP design process asks the same questions of administrative decisions: who is affected, what histories and power differences matter, who is absent from the room, and what constraints and mission commitments are in play. [^chunk:6b47b6d8-cbae-5d9f-b6f7-2ec82ea750b0] This is why inclusion is described not as an addition to the paradigm but as intrinsic to context and *cura personalis*: context that ignores disability, race, gender, economic condition, culture, language, religion, age, or prior education is simply an inaccurate context. [^chunk:514c92fa-878f-5bb4-b7dd-a668e30ffd19]
