# Governance, Leadership, and Apostolic Care in Jesuit Institutions

## Overview

Governance, Leadership, and Apostolic Care in Jesuit Institutions encompasses the structural, spiritual, and operational frameworks through which the Society of Jesus maintains its educational and apostolic missions. This domain addresses how Jesuit colleges, universities, and other apostolic works are directed, led, and sustained through integrated systems of religious governance, lay leadership partnership, and mission-centered discernment. It reflects the foundational tension between *cura personalis* (care of the individual) and *cura apostolica* (care of the mission)—two complementary dimensions rooted in the charism of Ignatius of Loyola that guide how Jesuit institutions serve both their members and their apostolic purposes.

## What This Section Covers

This comprehensive overview addresses seven interconnected dimensions of Jesuit institutional life:

### Apostolic Governance Structures and Leadership Roles

The Society of Jesus operates through a hierarchical yet mission-oriented governance structure that integrates multiple leadership roles. Major Superiors oversee entire provinces or regions and employ instruments such as annual visitations and accounts of conscience to ensure care of the life-mission. Local Superiors occupy critical positions within this network, strengthening members in their apostolic missions and cooperating with Directors of Work to foster Ignatian and Jesuit identity. Directors of Work—increasingly lay men and women, diocesan priests, and religious—serve as leaders of apostolic institutions while working in conjunction with the Society's governance structures.

### Cura Personalis and Cura Apostolica: Ignatian Care Concepts

These two complementary dimensions of Jesuit life represent a proper tension within the charism and way of proceeding of the Society of Jesus. *Cura personalis* (care of the individual person) and *cura apostolica* (apostolic care or care of the mission) gain particular importance in contemporary contexts characterized by cooperation with lay colleagues and new forms of governance. Although the formal terminology is relatively recent—appearing first in Fr. Ledóchowski's 1934 instruction on universities in the U.S. Assistancy and revised by Fr. Janssens in 1948—these concepts derive from lived experience deeply embedded in Ignatius of Loyola's spiritual journey, his Autobiography, the Spiritual Exercises, and the Constitutions.

### Leadership and Governance in Jesuit Colleges and Universities

Jesuit colleges and universities feature a distinctive dual-leadership model in which university presidents serve as Directors of the Apostolic Work while Jesuit community rectors lead the religious community. Presidents may be Jesuits or lay Catholics committed to the mission and are appointed by boards of trustees. When appointed, presidents should be formally "missioned" by their provincial to clarify the connection between the presidency and the broader apostolic mission of the Society. The rector, by contrast, leads the Jesuit community in its corporate animation of the apostolate rather than serving as a university administrator.

### Catholic and Jesuit Mission Identity in Higher Education

The defining mission of Jesuit colleges and universities centers on "the education and formation of our students for the sake of the kind of persons they become and their wide influence for good in society." This identity is maintained through commitment to highly qualified faculty exercising academic freedom, pursuit of truth, and the exercise of an intellectual apostolate vital to the Society's tradition. These twenty-eight U.S. Jesuit institutions identify as Catholic colleges and universities and operate within frameworks established by documents such as *Ex Corde Ecclesiae*.

### Collaboration and Governance Among Jesuit Institutions

The Association of Jesuit Colleges and Universities (AJCU) serves as the primary coordinating body for collaboration among U.S. Jesuit institutions. These institutions maintain formal structures for collaborative mission formation, including special retreats, seminars in Ignatian spirituality, and regional and national programs such as the AJCU Seminar on Higher Education Leadership and the Ignatian Colleagues Program. Full responsibility for policy, governance, and operation of each institution rests with its board of directors, trustees, or regents, though these boards operate within the broader context of Jesuit apostolic alignment.

### Mission Priority Examen and Self-Evaluation Processes

The Mission Priority Examen (MPE) is a comprehensive institutional process designed to help Jesuit colleges and universities reaffirm their Jesuit Catholic identity and establish strategic priorities. Initiated by the Superior General and coordinated by a joint committee of Provincials and University Presidents, the MPE invites entire institutional communities to engage in honest discernment by addressing fundamental questions: "Do you want to continue to be a Jesuit, Catholic University? If so, what are the two to four mission goals (and accompanying strategies) that you will prioritize for the next few years?" The process serves as a mechanism for naming institutional strengths, successes, struggles, and goals while honoring the voices and experiences of each community member.

### Jesuit Presence and Leadership Development in Institutional Culture

Jesuit presence operates at multiple levels through active community members, formation programs for lay leaders, and intentional hiring practices prioritizing mission alignment. Jesuit faculty, administrators, campus leaders, and campus ministers provide visible witness to the institution's Catholic and Jesuit identity. Universities are called to maintain strong cohorts of capable Jesuits contributing meaningfully to institutional life, vibrant Jesuit communities on campus, and open communication between Jesuit provincial superiors, university leadership, and boards of trustees. A significant contemporary tension exists as the number of Jesuits continues to decline while institutions seek to develop lay leadership capable of sustaining and advancing the Jesuit mission.

### Universal Apostolic Preferences and Strategic Institutional Direction

Universal Apostolic Preferences represent fundamental strategic orientations confirmed through General Congregations of the Society of Jesus, shaping how Jesuit institutions align their priorities with the broader mission of the Catholic Church and the Ignatian charism. Very Rev. Arturo Sosa, S.J., Superior General, has emphasized that these preferences constitute "basic orientations for our life-mission in the next ten years" and open "a new stage of our journey towards a better understanding and practice of the care of mission and people." The Mission Priority Examen operationalizes these universal preferences by requiring each institution to engage in discernment about its Jesuit Catholic identity and strategic commitments.

## Key Principles and Tensions

This domain of Jesuit institutional life reflects several foundational principles and creative tensions:

- **Integration of Religious and Lay Leadership**: Jesuit institutions increasingly rely on lay leaders in roles historically held by Jesuits, requiring new forms of governance that honor both religious charism and professional expertise.

- **Balance Between Mission and Institutional Independence**: While Jesuit colleges and universities operate as independent academic enterprises with autonomous boards of trustees, they remain apostolic works of the Society of Jesus and the Catholic Church.

- **Care of Persons and Care of Mission**: The complementary dimensions of *cura personalis* and *cura apostolica* require governance structures that simultaneously attend to the formation and accompaniment of individuals and the integrity of apostolic work.

- **Discernment as Governance Tool**: The use of Ignatian discernment processes—exemplified in the Mission Priority Examen—reflects the Society's conviction that institutional decisions should be rooted in spiritual reflection and communal wisdom rather than technical expertise alone.

- **Sustainability of Jesuit Identity**: The declining number of Jesuits in universities raises fundamental questions about how Jesuit identity and mission can be sustained and transmitted through primarily lay-led institutions.
