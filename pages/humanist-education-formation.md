## What is Humanist Education in the Jesuit Tradition?

Humanist education within the Jesuit tradition represents a distinctive pedagogical approach rooted in Christian humanism and the Society of Jesus's charism. Jesuit educational humanism is characterized by its commitment to supporting the growth of each person as a unique individual while simultaneously fostering their capacity to transcend self-interest and embrace responsibility for all humankind.[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b]

The core principle is that education should care for individuals in their particularity while helping them "go beyond themselves, can take up responsibility for all humankind, and open themselves up to the transcendent."[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b] This approach fundamentally differs from education that emphasizes only professional development; instead, Jesuit universities place overall human formation at the center of their educational mission.[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b]

The search for truth is central to Jesuit educational philosophy. A Jesuit university understands itself as "a community of spiritual concern and dedicated to seeking truth," operating from the conviction that "truth is something humans can really access."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f] This commitment extends beyond mere intellectual development; it involves recognizing that knowledge is always developing and persisting in the perpetual search for truth.[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

### Individual Dignity and Personal Formation

Humanist education centers on the principle of *cura personalis*—care for the individual person. This principle requires that universities look beyond professional credentials to recognize and nurture the human qualities of their graduates.[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b] Jesuit institutions aspire to be recognized "not only for their ability to work at high standards in a competitive market, but also for their human qualities."[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b]

This commitment to individual dignity is inseparable from the formation of wise persons. Wisdom, as understood in the Jesuit tradition, is not merely the accumulation of knowledge but "a superior, comprehensive, profound and transforming kind of knowledge."[^chunk:625d15bd-547d-5dae-9c1a-63c55f3d2ea0] It leads individuals to empathy, compassion toward all human beings, and respect for nature as God's gift.[^chunk:625d15bd-547d-5dae-9c1a-63c55f3d2ea0]

## Preparing Students for Global Citizenship and Intercultural Engagement

### Understanding World Citizenship

Jesuit universities explicitly aim to prepare students to become world citizens and intercultural agents. Citizenship, in this understanding, means "recognizing that we are part of a complex set of relationships with other people, and that together we are community."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f] World citizens recognize that each individual has "an essential and active part to play" in the broader human community and understand themselves as part of something greater than individual interests.[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

Becoming a world citizen involves adopting a perspective oriented toward "the common good and taking personal responsibility for the res publica—the things that concern and benefit a community as a whole."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f] Crucially, this perspective requires recognizing "that other people have equal rights and equal human dignity too."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

### Cultural Diversity and Intercultural Formation

Jesuit universities distinguish between globalization and what might be termed "mundialization" or universalization. While globalization can homogenize behavior and culture across the world, reducing cultural diversity, mundialization "recognizes the creative value of cultural diversity."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f] The latter represents "increased interaction among different cultural groups who nonetheless share a common vision for all humanity."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

Educating for world citizenship requires fostering what might be called intercultural competence at multiple levels. Jesuit institutions seek to educate people who are:

- **Inculturated**: "intelligent about and conscious of their own culture" and aware of membership in the human community[^chunk:1ea6cd96-ae96-54c4-a187-ec09f3e1cd1d]
- **Multicultural**: capable of "joyfully accept[ing] the cultures of other human beings" and relating to them[^chunk:1ea6cd96-ae96-54c4-a187-ec09f3e1cd1d]
- **Intercultural**: whose "lives are enriched by the span of cultures that includes their own"[^chunk:1ea6cd96-ae96-54c4-a187-ec09f3e1cd1d]

This intercultural formation addresses a pressing challenge of our era. As the General Congregation of the Society of Jesus noted in 2008: "We live in a world of many religions and cultures. The erosion of traditional religious beliefs and the tendency to homogenize culture has resulted in greater religious fundamentalism."[^chunk:1ea6cd96-ae96-54c4-a187-ec09f3e1cd1d] Intercultural encounter, enriched by spiritual experience as a dimension of culture, can help overcome fundamentalism in its various manifestations.[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

### University as Platform for Encounter

Jesuit universities function as unique spaces for intercultural and interreligious engagement. The Society of Jesus recognizes universities as "exceptionally effective platforms for putting into practice the mission to foster social justice and environmental sustainability through encounter with different cultures and religions – a mission that comes from the Gospel and is inspired by it."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

Universities are positioned as "diverse environments where people can dialogue about and come to understand historical, human, and intellectual changes deeply" and as "special arenas for practicing freedom."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f] This freedom extends to seeking and finding ways of transforming society through research and teaching.[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

## The Relationship Between Personal Formation and Social Responsibility

### Personal Formation as Foundation for Social Commitment

In Jesuit pedagogy, personal formation and social responsibility are inseparably linked. The education of whole persons—not merely the acquisition of professional skills—creates the foundation for meaningful social engagement. Jesuit universities seek to form "persons of integrity who will take up responsibility for themselves, for others, and for the earth that we all inhabit."[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b]

This integrated approach recognizes that authentic social responsibility emerges from deep personal formation. Individuals must develop the spiritual and intellectual depth necessary to understand people in their particular contexts and to recognize themselves as part of the world's cultural diversity.[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b]

### The Call to Social Justice and Reconciliation

Jesuit education explicitly seeks to nurture what might be called a "preferential option for the poor." Universities are understood as ventures aimed at transforming society, institutions that generate life in abundance.[^chunk:4e41a659-1dff-5d8e-99b1-1be2c442e617] This transformation involves confronting obstacles to reconciliation and developing solutions to complex social problems.

Crucially, Jesuit pedagogy aims to enable students to hear "the call to serve society as a personal commitment."[^chunk:1ea6cd96-ae96-54c4-a187-ec09f3e1cd1d] Direct involvement in the political sphere—understood as "putting oneself at the service of reconciliation and justice"—is presented as both necessary and complex.[^chunk:1ea6cd96-ae96-54c4-a187-ec09f3e1cd1d] Educating political and social leaders is explicitly identified as "one of the most important things we can do to improve societies across the globe."[^chunk:1ea6cd96-ae96-54c4-a187-ec09f3e1cd1d]

### Environmental Responsibility as Personal and Collective Duty

Social responsibility in Jesuit education extends to environmental stewardship. The vision is to help people develop "responsibility for the earth that we all inhabit."[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b] This reflects a broader understanding of reconciliation that includes reconciliation with the natural world as part of the mission of justice and healing.

The formation of consciences extends to creating what has been called "a culture of protecting the vulnerable," which includes safeguarding the environment as part of caring for our "common home."[^chunk:4e41a659-1dff-5d8e-99b1-1be2c442e617]

### The Integration of Wisdom and Action

Jesuit pedagogy understands that intellectual work must ultimately serve human flourishing and social transformation. Intellectual depth and the pursuit of wisdom are not ends in themselves but are meant to contribute to "making the world a better place to live."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

This understanding is captured in the concept of intellectual work as ministry. Jesuit universities are called to be "a form of ministry — that is, a way of announcing the Good News of the Gospel more effectively and of becoming aware of God's presence in the world and the action of his Spirit in history."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f] Through this ministry, institutions join in the creative work of Christ and help spread freedom.[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

The ministry of intellectual work is fundamentally characterized by its openness: it is "carried out, as it were, in the open air, i.e., not shut away in an office or the comfort of its own certainties and convictions."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f] It engages in dialogue with other disciplines, allows its own position to be enriched by other perspectives and worldviews, and is lived "as a mission, i.e. as having been sent to help build a freer world."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

### Discernment as Integrative Practice

The Jesuit pedagogical tradition employs discernment as a means of integrating personal formation with social responsibility. Through discernment, educators and students learn to recognize where God is active in the world and to choose "what is for the greater glory of God, i.e. the human person fully alive."[^chunk:6690fa56-9d79-5a30-8924-f7fe5f1f3d4f]

## The Humanist Tradition as Response to Our Time

Jesuit educational humanism represents a living tradition that must respond creatively to contemporary challenges. As institutions embody their identity, they must balance loyalty to tradition with creative responses to the signs of the times. The humanist tradition is characterized as:

- **Inculturated**: having roots in every place[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b]
- **Dialogical**: about connecting with other cultures and traditions[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b]
- **Intercultural**: enriched through encounter between cultures[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b]

Through these characteristics, Jesuit educational humanism "weaves together the strands of personal and local relationships with the complex realities of the world."[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b]

The ultimate goal is to form what one Jesuit theologian calls people who are "poor with Spirit, who freely give of their very selves and who enrich everyone's existence by making life more human."[^chunk:516aa191-9958-510f-8f23-29fd7f81246b] These are individuals formed not only for personal success but for the transformation of society toward greater justice, reconciliation, and the flourishing of all humanity.
