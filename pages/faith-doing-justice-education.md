# Faith That Does Justice: Jesuit Educational Mission

## Understanding "Faith That Does Justice"

"Faith that does justice" represents a central commitment of Jesuit universities, expressing the inseparable unity between service of faith and promotion of justice.[^chunk:6d57e7d9-44bd-5906-8506-1269f2caa002] This concept emerged from General Congregation 32, which redefined the Jesuit mission as the "service of faith and the promotion of justice."[^chunk:253fa195-ca17-5646-9405-e38cbe6f3b98] Rather than viewing faith and justice as separate concerns, Jesuit institutions understand them as two dimensions of a single mission: "The way to faith and the way to justice are inseparable ways."[^chunk:253fa195-ca17-5646-9405-e38cbe6f3b98]

For Jesuit universities, this commitment means that institutions must be "faithful to both the noun 'university' and to the adjective 'Jesuit.'"[^chunk:253fa195-ca17-5646-9405-e38cbe6f3b98] To be a university requires dedication to research, teaching, and service; to be Jesuit requires acting in harmony with the demands of faith and justice. As articulated by Ignacio Ellacuría, "it is the nature of every university to be a social force, and it is the calling of a Jesuit university to take conscious responsibility for being such a force for faith and justice."[^chunk:253fa195-ca17-5646-9405-e38cbe6f3b98]

## Pedro Arrupe's Vision of "Men and Women for Others"

Pedro Arrupe, Superior General of the Society of Jesus, articulated the foundational concept of Jesuit education as forming "men and women for others."[^chunk:a664b19a-af4f-5620-9c55-351d7f3b3e16] Speaking to the Tenth International Congress of Jesuit Alumni of Europe in Valencia in 1973, Arrupe acknowledged that past Jesuit education had not adequately prepared students for the demands of justice that the Church now recognized as essential to its mission.[^chunk:a664b19a-af4f-5620-9c55-351d7f3b3e16]

Arrupe defined the paramount objective of Jesuit education as forming graduates who would "give himself to others in love—love, which is his definitive and all-embracing dimension, that which gives meaning to all his other dimensions."[^chunk:a664b19a-af4f-5620-9c55-351d7f3b3e16] This vision positioned education for justice as central rather than peripheral to Jesuit pedagogy. Arrupe emphasized that "men cannot even conceive of love of God which does not include love for the least of their neighbors; men completely convinced that love of God which does not issue in justice for men is a farce."[^chunk:a664b19a-af4f-5620-9c55-351d7f3b3e16]

Importantly, Arrupe grounded this transformative potential in Ignatian spirituality. He argued that the Society possessed the necessary tools for reform through "the spirit of constantly seeking the will of God" and "sensitiveness to the Spirit which enables us to recognize where, in what direction, Christ is calling us at different periods of history."[^chunk:a664b19a-af4f-5620-9c55-351d7f3b3e16] Only through becoming a man or woman for others does one achieve full humanity: a person becomes "the 'spiritual' man of Saint Paul...the man filled with the Spirit; and we know whose Spirit that is: the Spirit of Christ, who gave his life for the salvation of the world."[^chunk:0ed584b5-2836-5ae9-a847-ffd2fec996c3]

## The Four C's of Jesuit Formation

Contemporary Jesuit universities have articulated student formation through four complementary qualities: **conscientious, competent, compassionate, and committed** persons.[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689]

### Conscientious
Conscientious persons understand life as a gift for which they are grateful and develop their own personal freedom from this conviction. They recognize the dignity of others and seek for them the highest level of personal realization. They feel responsible for the world in which they live and feel called to care for it and improve it, grounding this responsibility in God as the Love that creates human beings in his own image and likeness.[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689]

### Competent
Competent persons are able to offer the quality service expected of them and are qualified to carry out the work for which they have been prepared. Competency levels are specific to each educational stage and vary according to societal determinations of what should be acquired at each stage.[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689]

### Compassionate
Compassionate persons possess the sensitivity required to perceive the needs of others and respond to them. They become brothers and sisters to others, transforming their own existence in the process. They feel responsible for others and join with them in loving their lives, celebrating their joys, easing their needs, and generating hope.[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689]

### Committed
Committed persons devote their whole persons—intelligence, will, and feelings—to making the world more just. They seek creative solutions and freely commit their talents and energies to the cause of changing unjust structures, institutions, and laws. They take on public responsibility for the sake of promoting the common good.[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689]

## The Four C's and Social Justice

These four qualities work together as an integrated framework for social justice education. The formation of conscientious students establishes the moral foundation; competence ensures graduates can effectively serve others; compassion transforms ethical understanding into genuine human encounter; and commitment channels these qualities into sustained action for structural change.

The Jesuit educational model recognizes that "the goal of education, especially Christian education, is more than information. It is wisdom."[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689] This wisdom encompasses not merely professional development but the formation of "responsible, committed citizens" and an opening to "the transcendent dimensions of life."[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689]

Father Peter-Hans Kolvenbach affirmed that "the goal of Jesuit education is the formation of men and women for others, people of competence, conscience and compassionate commitment."[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689] The evaluation criterion for Jesuit universities ultimately measures success not by institutional rankings but by "what the students become."[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689] Universities must assess the human qualities graduates develop in their "psychological, moral, and spiritual lives," asking "what type of persons do our students become and what are they doing with their lives?"[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689]

## Formation of Conscience and Social Reality

The formation of conscience requires sustained engagement with social reality. Students must develop "attentive intelligence that is nourished by imagination and motivated by interest and desire within a shared culture."[^chunk:598bd33e-24eb-5870-bd1f-4ca21e532689] Direct contact with poor communities transforms ethical understanding: "Personal involvement with innocent suffering, with the injustice others suffer, is the catalyst for solidarity which then gives rise to intellectual inquiry and moral reflection."[^chunk:250e2d93-005e-57db-ae12-12926dd08952]

Jesuit universities employ various immersion programs to facilitate this transformation. Service and insertion programs are integrated with intellectual study, supervised by experienced professors, and include reflective components where students examine both external social structures and their own inner movements—a characteristic practice of Ignatian spirituality.[^chunk:250e2d93-005e-57db-ae12-12926dd08952]
